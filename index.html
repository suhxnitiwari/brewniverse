    02 · Bean Anatomy

    A coffee bean is a seed

    Before it’s roasted, it’s wrapped in layers of fruit and husk. Tap any layer to learn what it is, or peel the cherry the way a farm does.

    Tap a layer

        Peaberry

        In roughly 5–10% of cherries only one seed develops. It grows round instead of flat. Hit the Peaberry button to see one.

        The center cut

        The S-shaped crease down a roasted bean is where the edges of the seed folded in as it grew.

        It’s a fruit

        Coffee grows on Coffea shrubs, which are in the same plant family as gardenias. The “beans” are the seeds of its cherries.

        03 · Beans & Roasts

        Not all beans are the same

        There are over 130 species of coffee plant, but nearly everything you drink is one of two: Arabica or Robusta. Tap a bean to compare them and see where the names come from.

        How coffee names fit together

        1. SpeciesThe kind of plantArabica
        2. VarietyA breed of that plantCaturra
        3. OriginWhere it grewColombia
        4. GradeBean size or qualitySupremo
        5. RoastHow dark it’s cookedMedium

        So a bag of “Colombian Supremo, medium roast” is Arabica (the plant), often the Caturra or Castillo variety, grown in Colombia, with extra-large beans, roasted medium. “Arabica” and “Colombian” aren’t competing types. One is what it is, the other is where it’s from.

        Coffees by origin

        Take a species (almost always Arabica), grow it in a particular place, and it picks up that place’s flavor from the soil, altitude, weather and how the farmers process it. That’s why bags say “Colombian,” “Kenya AA” or “Sumatra.” Same plant, different home, different taste.

        Rare & wild species

        Coffee plants you’ll almost never find in a café, though a couple might matter a lot as the planet warms.

        Varieties

        Inside each species there are varieties, the way Granny Smith and Fuji are both apples. Almost all Arabica traces back to two lines that left Yemen, Typica and Bourbon. Some ripen red, others yellow, orange or pink.

          Roast levels

          The same green bean can end up any of these colors. Darker isn’t stronger, it just tastes more like the roast and less like the bean. Tap a pile.

          05 · Roastery

          Turn up the heat

          Hold the burner to roast a green bean. Listen for first crack around 196 °C and second crack around 224 °C. Or just drag the thermometer.

          Myth: dark roast has more caffeine

          Roasting barely touches caffeine. Dark beans lose more water and get lighter and puffier, so a scoop of light roast has a little more caffeine, while by weight dark roast edges ahead. Either way the difference is tiny. “Strong” tasting isn’t the same as more caffeine.

          06 · Grind Guide

          Size matters

          The faster the brew, the finer the grind. Smaller pieces mean more surface for water to pull flavor from. Slide it or pick your brewer.
